\chapter{Writing a Technical or Project Report}
\label{chap:report}
\index{technical report}\index{executive summary}\index{recommendation}\index{document control}

A technical report helps a defined audience make, record, or review a decision.
Its value lies not in a formal cover but in the traceable path from purpose and
evidence to findings, limitations, recommendations, owners, and next actions.

\begin{learningoutcomes}
  \item Define audience, decision, scope, status, and handling requirements.
  \item Build a cover, document-control record, executive summary, and body.
  \item Separate observations, interpretations, recommendations, and decisions.
  \item Present risk, uncertainty, actions, and evidence registers clearly.
  \item Maintain version, approval, distribution, and archival information.
  \item Compile and adapt the independent technical-report template.
\end{learningoutcomes}

\section{Begin with the decision}

Ask who will read the report, what they must decide, when they must decide it,
and which evidence is within scope. A report without a decision context often
becomes a chronological diary of work.

Write a one-sentence commissioning statement:

\begin{quote}
This report evaluates [evidence] so that [decision owner] can decide [specific
decision] by [date], subject to [important boundary].
\end{quote}

That sentence controls relevance. Useful material outside the boundary can be
recorded as future work rather than crowding the main argument.

\begin{pausepredict}
Two audiences need the same results: a technical reviewer must reproduce the
analysis, while an executive must choose whether to fund a validation study.
Which information belongs in the executive summary, the methods, and an
appendix? Identify what both audiences still need in the main findings.
\end{pausepredict}

\section{The cover is controlled metadata}

A report cover commonly identifies:

\begin{itemize}
  \item exact title and report or project identifier;
  \item client, sponsor, or decision owner;
  \item authoring organisation and responsible authors;
  \item issue date, version, and status;
  \item distribution or classification marking where applicable.
\end{itemize}

Treat ``Draft'', ``Approved'', ``Public'', and ``Confidential'' as controlled
states, not visual decorations. The cover, headers, filename, document register,
and repository record must agree. Do not apply a restriction merely because an
old template contains one, and do not remove one without authority.

\begin{commonerror}
\textbf{Symptom:} a PDF named \file{final.pdf} has ``Draft'' in the footer and a
different version in the document-control table.

\textbf{Cause:} status metadata was typed in independent locations.

\textbf{Repair:} define shared commands for report ID, version, date, and status;
generate the release filename from a controlled convention where practical;
then add a pre-release consistency check.
\end{commonerror}

\section{Document control and distribution}

The version table records meaningful issued states, not every keystroke. A row
should state the version, date, change, author, reviewer or approver as required.
Version control preserves fine-grained source history; the issued report table
records milestones relevant to recipients.

A distribution statement names authorised recipients or the public repository,
handling rules, and superseded versions. If a report changes after approval,
issue a new controlled version rather than silently replacing the archived PDF.

\section{Write the executive summary last}

Although it appears first, write the executive summary after the findings and
recommendations have stabilised. It should enable the intended decision without
requiring the reader to reconstruct the entire analysis. A strong pattern is:

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item[Decision required] The precise choice and decision owner.
  \item[Evidence and finding] The most decision-relevant result, with scale and
  uncertainty.
  \item[Recommendation] The action proposed and its conditions.
  \item[Principal risks] Consequences, uncertainty, and unresolved constraints.
  \item[Next step] Owner, evidence of completion, and due date.
\end{description}

Do not introduce numbers that cannot be located in the main report. Avoid an
executive summary that merely promises what later sections contain.

\section{Purpose, scope, and assumptions}

Define the system, population, sites, dates, configurations, data, and questions
covered. State exclusions. An ``out of scope'' section prevents reasonable but
unsupported generalisation.

List assumptions that affect interpretation, then distinguish them from facts.
For each consequential assumption, record how it was checked or how a decision
would change if it failed. A vague assumption list at the end of an appendix
does not manage risk.

\section{Methods and assurance}

Methods should allow a competent reviewer to assess the work. Record data
sources, collection or simulation, inclusion and exclusion rules, equipment and
calibration where applicable, preprocessing, equations, software, uncertainty,
review, and deviations from the plan.

Use stable labels to connect methods and findings. If the report states a paired
difference, define its direction once:

\begin{latexcode}{Direction made explicit}
\begin{equation}
  d_i = B_i-A_i.
  \label{eq:report-difference}
\end{equation}
Positive values therefore indicate that method B is higher.
\end{latexcode}

Without the last sentence, a later ``positive bias'' can be interpreted in two
directions.

\begin{scienceinpractice}
An engineering qualification report can separate test execution, data
reduction, acceptance criteria, results, non-conformances, and disposition. A
failed criterion must remain visible even if an authorised decision accepts the
risk; the report should not rewrite failure as success.
\end{scienceinpractice}

\section{Findings are not recommendations}

Keep four statement types distinct:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Observation:} what the data or inspection records show.
  \item \textbf{Interpretation:} what the observation means under stated
        assumptions.
  \item \textbf{Recommendation:} what the author proposes should happen.
  \item \textbf{Decision:} what the authorised owner has chosen.
\end{enumerate}

For example, ``Method B averaged \SI{0.18}{\milli\metre} higher'' is an
observation. ``The offset is acceptable'' is an interpretation requiring a
tolerance. ``Proceed to validation'' is a recommendation. ``The programme board
approved validation on [date]'' is a decision with a record.

\section{Use tables and figures for decisions}

A decision table can connect evidence to consequence:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{.23\textwidth}XX@{}}
  \toprule
  Finding & Decision significance & Required response \\
  \midrule
  Mean paired offset observed & Possible calibration difference & Compare with approved tolerance \\
  Tolerance not prespecified & Acceptability cannot be concluded & Obtain decision-owner approval \\
  Data are simulated & No operational evidence & Conduct controlled empirical study \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Figures should answer a question that the text names. Captions must identify
simulated or illustrative data, define reference lines, units, and uncertainty,
and remain legible when printed in grayscale. Avoid dashboard colour without a
semantic key.

\section{Risk and uncertainty}

Risk statements should name cause, event, and consequence. A practical register
may include likelihood, impact, existing controls, owner, action, and residual
risk, but scales must be defined. Numbers that resemble precision without a
calibrated basis are not automatically better than transparent categories.

Statistical uncertainty and project risk are related but different. A
confidence interval describes an inferential procedure under assumptions;
``high operational risk'' combines probability, consequence, and context. Do
not translate one into the other without an explicit model.

\begin{goodpractice}
Put acceptance criteria in the protocol or report before looking at outcomes.
If a criterion changes, record the rationale, authority, date, and effect rather
than overwriting the earlier state.
\end{goodpractice}

\section{Recommendations must be executable}

A recommendation needs an action verb, owner, completion evidence, and where
appropriate priority and due date. Compare:

\begin{quote}
Weak: Further study is recommended.

Actionable: The study lead shall submit a paired validation protocol defining
the acceptance tolerance and calibration controls to the decision owner by
[date]; approval of that protocol is the completion evidence.
\end{quote}

Avoid assigning an owner who has not accepted responsibility. If authority has
not been confirmed, retain a placeholder and flag it before issue.

\section{Limitations and non-conformances}

Limitations define the boundary of a claim; they are not ritual self-criticism.
State their likely direction or consequence where possible. A small convenience
sample may limit generalisability; missing calibration records may make the
cause of an offset indeterminate.

Record deviations and non-conformances with identifiers, evidence, impact,
disposition, authority, and closure status. Never remove an adverse result from
a final report merely because corrective action was later completed; document
the sequence.

\section{Appendices and evidence registers}

Appendices can hold detailed protocols, data dictionaries, calculation checks,
large tables, drawings, approval records, and a release checklist. Every
appendix should be cited from the main report when it supports a claim.

An evidence register gives each object an ID, description, version, location,
owner, and access condition. The issued PDF should refer to immutable or
versioned evidence rather than a mutable personal folder.

\section{Release and archival workflow}

Before issue:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item complete independent technical and editorial review;
  \item resolve comments or record authorised dispositions;
  \item reproduce headline findings from source evidence;
  \item verify status, identifiers, dates, classification, and approval;
  \item build and inspect a clean PDF;
  \item create a file manifest and checksum if required;
  \item place the release in the controlled repository;
  \item notify authorised recipients and supersede earlier versions visibly.
\end{enumerate}

Keep the signed or approved record separate if inserting a signature image would
weaken the security or validity of the approval process. Follow organisational
policy rather than inventing a scanned-signature convention.

\documentclass{report}
\usepackage[margin=28mm]{geometry}
\usepackage{booktabs}

\title{Method Verification Report}
\author{Prepared for: Client or laboratory placeholder}
\date{2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Decision required:} approve, revise, or reject the proposed method.
\end{abstract}
\tableofcontents

\chapter{Objective}
Assess whether the method meets the stated acceptance criterion.

\chapter{Findings}
The simulated trial produced the following summary.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
  \toprule
  Criterion & Simulated result \\
  \midrule
  Mean recovery & 94.8\% \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\chapter{Recommendation}
Repeat the trial with an independent sample before adoption.
\end{document}
\end{latexcode}

\chaptersummary

A technical report is organised around a defined decision. Controlled metadata
identifies the report's status; an executive summary compresses the evidence and
recommendation; methods establish assurance; and findings remain distinct from
interpretations, recommendations, and authorised decisions. Action owners,
evidence registers, limitations, review, and archival controls make the report
operationally trustworthy as well as readable.

\chaptercheck
\begin{chapterchecklist}
  \item Audience, decision, scope, exclusions, and deadline are explicit.
  \item Report ID, version, date, status, and handling markings agree everywhere.
  \item Executive-summary values and conditions match the body.
  \item Methods, acceptance criteria, uncertainty, and deviations are traceable.
  \item Observations, interpretations, recommendations, and decisions are distinct.
  \item Every action has a realistic owner and completion evidence.
  \item The approved release and evidence manifest are archived immutably.
\end{chapterchecklist}
